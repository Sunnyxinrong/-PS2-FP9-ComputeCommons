\subsection{Critical-payment argument}
Fix bidder $i$'s eligibility, all rival bids and the public rule. For $q\geq1$, increasing $b_i$ weakly increases its rank. Let $t_i$ be the $q$th-highest rival score when $q$ rivals exist, and zero otherwise. The winning region is $b_i+ce_i>t_i$, together with any boundary assigned by the fixed tie order. Thus the nonnegative critical value is $\tau_i=\max(0,t_i-ce_i)$. Winning yields utility $v_i-\tau_i$ regardless of one's winning report; losing yields zero. If $v_i>\tau_i$, truthful reporting wins and no losing deviation improves utility. If $v_i<\tau_i$, truthful reporting loses and winning gives negative utility. At equality, either outcome gives zero utility. If the credit makes $\tau_i=0$, reporting truthfully never forces a negative payoff. For $q=0$, all utilities are zero. Consequently truthful reporting is weakly dominant, individually rational, and induces nonnegative payments. At $c=0$ this reduces to the unit-demand Vickrey benchmark \cite{vickrey,roughgarden}.

The proof is conditional on eligibility verification, unit demand, private values, enforceable payments and bid-independent tie-breaking. It neither implies a unique equilibrium nor predicts that every human bids truthfully. A bidder may submit several different reports that induce the same allocation and payment. Accordingly, the behavioral outcome of interest is profitable deviation or realized utility loss, not just numerical distance from value.

\subsection{Access threshold and comparative statics}
Let two ineligible incumbents have values $a\geq b>d$, and one eligible entrant value $d$, with incumbents preceding the entrant in ties. At $K=2,r=0$, the entrant wins iff $c>b-d$. At $r=1$, it wins iff $c>a-d$. A worker reservation raises the minimum strict winning credit by $a-b$ in this profile, because the entrant now competes with the highest incumbent instead of the lower one. This is a transparent order-statistic result for this environment, not a general theorem about all access policies.

At $a=10,b=9,d=6$, the strict thresholds are 3 and 4. With $r=0$ and $c\in(3,4]$, $A$ and $E$ win, their payments are 9 and $9-c$, and revenue is $18-c$. Baseline revenue is 12. At $c=4$ the resulting revenue is 14. Revenue is therefore not a monotone proxy for firm-value efficiency, and a credit does not necessarily reduce revenue. At $r=1,c=5$, $E$ pays $10-5=5$. The access comparison must hold capacity fixed when interpreting a credit effect. Moving from $(r,c)=(0,0)$ directly to $(1,5)$ mixes two interventions; the intermediate treatments in Table~\ref{tab:results} separate them.

For fixed $r$, report credit's firm-value opportunity cost as the top-$q$ uncredited value sum minus $V$. It equals 0, 3, 0 and 4 for the four displayed rules. Relative to the no-reservation baseline, the combined rule displaces 13 firm-value units, including 9 attributable to reducing commercial capacity and 4 to the credit conditional on that capacity. These figures exclude the value of the worker-directed evaluation. Neither the credit parameter nor revenue should be added to raw firm value as though it were an observed social benefit. Transfers are not new productive value.

\subsection{Eligibility stress and audit boundary}
Under $(r,c)=(1,5)$, truthful eligibility produces $E$ as winner and $u_A=0$. If $A$ can acquire an undeserved label, scores become $(15,9,11)$. $A$ wins, pays $11-5=6$, and earns $10-6=4$. The code's recorded eligible-slot count then equals one, although the true entrant receives zero slots. This distinction motivates auditing identities, not merely counting labeled winners.

Assume a false claim is detected after allocation with independent probability $a$, detection is perfect when audited, and an additional enforceable fine $L$ applies without changing the allocation or payment. The deviation's expected incremental gain is $4-aL$. Hence $aL\geq4$ weakly deters this particular deviation; at $L=10$, $a\geq0.4$. If audit cost per inspection is $k$, the associated expected inspection cost is $ak$. Credible enforcement, false positives, risk preferences, legal authority, optimal audit policy and other values are unresolved. The proposed practical safeguard is verification before bidding, with an appeal channel; the ex-post fine calculation diagnoses the missing institution rather than supplying a deployment policy.

\subsection{Language-independent algorithm}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Validate finite nonnegative values, bids and credit, public binary eligibility, and integer $0\leq r\leq K$.
\item Compute $q=\min(n,K-r)$ and each score $b_i+ce_i$.
\item Sort descending by score, resolving ties by fixed bidder index. Assign the first $q$ firms one window each.
\item Let $z$ be the highest losing score, or zero if everyone wins. Charge winning $i$ the amount $\max(0,z-ce_i)$; charge losers zero. If $q=0$, nobody pays.
\item Compute allocation, true-value utility, revenue, raw firm value, verified entrant access and worker-reserved capacity.
\item Repeat with identical values and capacity across rules. Independently enumerate feasible subsets on a small grid and check every tested unilateral deviation.
\end{enumerate}
For a winner, the highest losing score equals its $q$th-highest rival score. Sorting costs $O(n\log n)$; subsequent calculations cost $O(n)$. Exhaustive verification intentionally uses a slower independent subset objective on three firms.

\textbf{OpenVLA \cite{openvla}:} research question: generalist robot control from vision and language; method: a seven-billion-parameter VLA trained on robot demonstrations; validation: robot-task evaluation and adaptation studies. \textbf{This project:} proposes a resource-admission institution around a candidate VLA workload.
